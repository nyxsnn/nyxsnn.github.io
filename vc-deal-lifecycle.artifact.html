The venture deal lifecycle

From an LP's commitment to the carry that flows back.

A venture fund is a machine for routing one limited partner's capital through seven stages — sourcing, diligence, term sheet, dilution, follow-on, exit, and the waterfall home. This page walks all seven. Every figure here is either cited to a public source or computed live from inputs you control — nothing is an illustrative number dressed up as a real one.

§1 begins below ↓

Before we start — three corrections

The parts outsiders usually get backwards.

If you already work in the industry, skip ahead. If you are about to, fix these first.

Perceived

A good VC picks winners.

Actual

A VC builds a portfolio wide enough that one or two winners can return the entire fund. Across 2004–2018, roughly 65% of US venture investments failed to return their capital and about 1 in 20 returned more than 10×.

Perceived

The valuation is the deal.

Actual

The valuation sets the price. The term sheet sets the control — liquidation preference, pro-rata rights, the option-pool carve-out, and board composition decide outcomes the headline number never shows.

Perceived

Founders and investors want the same thing.

Actual

Both want a large outcome, but a fund needs a fund-returner. That pushes an investor toward the higher-variance path a founder might rather de-risk. Incentives align on direction, not on variance.

The sequence

Seven clauses. One dollar of committed capital, followed end to end.

Three of the seven are built as working models — move the inputs. The rest are the connective tissue that makes the models mean something.

§1

Sourcinginteractive

Deal flow in → one cheque out

A fund's returns are capped by what it sees and what it can win. Partners work inbound (warm intros, founders who came back) and outbound (theses, cold outreach, scouts). Almost everything is a “no” — the job is to make the few yeses count. Move the assumptions and watch how many companies the funnel has to chew through to produce a single investment.

§2

Diligence

Weeks spent trying to kill the deal

Between a partner meeting and a term sheet, the investor spends weeks trying to talk itself out. Customer calls, cohort retention, the existing cap table, key-person risk, the data room, references the founder didn't offer. Most companies that reach this point still don't get a term sheet — they fall out in diligence or in the partnership vote. The output isn't just yes/no; it's the specific risks the deal has to price around.

§3

Term Sheet & Valuation

Pricing the round, allocating control

A term sheet does two jobs. It prices the round — pre-money + new investment = post-money, and the investor's ownership is investment ÷ post-money. And it allocates control: a 1× non-participating liquidation preference, pro-rata rights to defend ownership later, a board seat, and a refreshed option pool that is carved out of the pre-money — so the pool dilutes the founders and existing holders, not the incoming investor. §4 shows exactly what that carve-out costs.

§5

Post-Investment

Money in is the start, not the finish

The wire clears and the work begins: a board seat, help shaping the next round's narrative, senior hires, customer and investor intros, and a running judgement about whether this is a company to double down on or to quietly stop supporting. Most of a partner's attention goes to the roughly one-fifth of the portfolio that is clearly working — the rest gets managed, not built.

§6

Follow-On

Reserves exist to defend the winners

Funds hold back capital — often 1× to 2× the initial cheque — specifically to buy their pro-rata in the breakout rounds of companies that are working. Exercising pro-rata in a hot Series B is how a 5% stake avoids decaying to 2% by exit. Reserves left unused are a drag on returns; reserves poured into the losers are worse.

§7

Exit & Waterfallinteractive

Splitting the proceeds between LPs and the GP

A company exits — acquisition or IPO — and the fund's share of the proceeds runs through the distribution waterfall. In the standard whole-fund European model: LPs get their capital back first, then a preferred return (the hurdle), then the GP “catches up” to its carry percentage, and everything after splits 80 / 20. Move fund size, proceeds, hurdle, and carry to see who ends up with what.
